What is this even about?

An oil company drills a long, skinny hole deep underground to reach oil and gas. As they drill, they desperately want to know what kind of rock the drill is passing through right now, because the oil only lives in certain rock. Aim wrong and you drill through worthless rock and waste millions.

The problem: you can't see underground. You only get a few indirect measurements. This challenge is about taking those measurements and guessing the rock the drill is in — automatically, with a computer.

The whole task in one line

“Here are some clues about a hole in the ground. Tell me which rock layer the hole is in, all the way along.”

Step 1 · The mental picture

The ground is a tilted layer cake

Picture this Imagine a giant layer cake buried underground — sponge, then jam, then cream, then sponge again, stacked for thousands of feet. Each layer is a different type of rock. The layers were laid down flat over millions of years, but the ground has since tilted, so the cake sits at a slight slant.

That's basically what's under our feet: stacked sheets of rock, each one different, often gently tilted. Geologists call each sheet a formation or layer.

New word

Formation / layer = one sheet of a particular rock type in the cake. In this dataset the layers have short code names like ANCC, BUDA, EGFDU — just treat them as “the jam layer,” “the cream layer,” etc.

Step 2 · The hole

A well is a straw pushed through the cake

Picture this A well is like pushing a very long straw into the cake. Older wells go straight down. Modern wells go down a bit and then turn sideways, traveling horizontally for a mile or more so they pass through a lot of the good layer.

This challenge is all about those horizontal wells — the ones that turn and travel sideways. The sideways part is called the lateral.

New words

Wellbore = the actual hole the drill makes (the straw). Horizontal well = a well that turns and runs sideways. Lateral = the long sideways section of that well.

Step 3 · The one number we care about

“How deep into the cake am I?” = TVT

At any point along the straw, the single most useful thing to know is: how far down into the layer cake are we? That depth-into-the-cake is the number this whole contest is about. It's called TVT.

The star of the show

TVT (True Vertical Thickness) = how deep you are measured straight down through the layers, regardless of how far sideways the straw has wandered. If you know the TVT, you know exactly which layer you're in. TVT is the thing we must predict.

Why two kinds of “depth”? Imagine walking down a wheelchair ramp vs. a ladder. You might walk 100 feet along the ramp but only drop 10 feet down. The “length you walked” and the “height you dropped” are different numbers. Wells are the same — so geologists track both (next step).

Step 4 · Two rulers

Length-along-the-straw vs. depth-into-the-cake

NamePlain meaningRamp analogy
MD (Measured Depth)How much straw you've pushed in — total distance traveled along the hole.Feet walked along the ramp
TVTHow deep into the layer cake you are.Height you actually dropped

So as the lateral travels sideways, MD keeps climbing steadily (more straw used), while TVT wiggles up and down depending on whether the straw is rising or sinking through the layers.

Step 5 · The magic clue

Gamma Ray: every rock layer has a “glow fingerprint”

Picture this Every rock type is naturally, very faintly radioactive — some glow a little more, some a little less. A sensor on the drill measures this faint glow continuously. So each layer of the cake has its own glow level, like a barcode stripe: jam glows high, cream glows low, sponge glows medium.

This glow measurement is called Gamma Ray, or GR. It's the single most powerful clue we have, because the pattern of glow as you move tells you which layers you're crossing.

New word

GR (Gamma Ray) = a number measuring the natural “glow” (radioactivity) of the rock at each point. Different layers have different, recognizable GR values. Think of it as a fingerprint scanner for rock. (Occasionally the sensor misses a reading, so some GR values are blank / NaN — “not a number.”)

Step 6 · The cheat sheet

The “typewell” is the answer key

Picture this Before drilling sideways, someone drilled a plain straight-down hole nearby and carefully recorded the glow (GR) at every depth, plus the name of the layer at every depth. That straight-down hole is a complete, labeled fingerprint catalog of the cake: “at this depth the glow is X and it's the jam layer,” all the way down.

That reference hole is called the typewell. It's your cheat sheet. Because it goes straight down, its TVT (depth-into-cake) is never in doubt — it's known everywhere.

New word

Typewell = a nearby straight-down well where we already know everything: the glow (GR), the depth (TVT), and the layer name (Geology) at every point. Each sideways well is paired with one typewell to use as its reference.

Step 7 · The trick that makes it solvable

Matching fingerprints tells you the depth

Here's the clever part. The sideways well also measures glow (GR) as it goes. If, at some point, the sideways well reads a glow pattern that matches the “jam layer” pattern in the answer key, then we know the straw must currently be in the jam layer — so we know its depth-into-the-cake (TVT)!

The core idea

Match the sideways well's glow pattern against the straight-down answer key → and you've figured out which layer it's in → which gives you the TVT.

And the direction the pattern plays tells you which way the straw is moving:

In plain terms

It's like recognizing a song. If you hear the chorus played forward, you're moving forward through the song; if you hear it backward, you're rewinding. The glow pattern is the song; the answer key is the original recording.

Step 8 · The boring-but-important case

Flat glow = staying in one layer

Sometimes the straw runs along a single layer instead of crossing layers. Then the glow barely changes — and that tells you the depth (TVT) is staying roughly the same.

  • 📈 Glow plays the pattern forward → going deeper (TVT up)
  • 📉 Glow plays the pattern backward → coming up (TVT down)
  • ➡️ Glow stays flat → same depth (TVT constant)

Step 10 · The actual challenge setup

You're given half the answers — predict the other half

Picture this Imagine driving with GPS. For the first part of the trip the GPS works perfectly and shows exactly where you are. Then the GPS dies. For the rest of the trip you have to figure out your position yourself, using the road signs (the glow pattern) and a map (the answer key).

That “moment the GPS dies” is called the Prediction Start, or PS. Before PS, you're told the depth (TVT) at every point. After PS, you must predict it.

New word

PS (Prediction Start) = the point along the well where the known answers stop and your job begins. The column TVT_input is just “TVT, but only filled in up to PS” — it's the part of the trip where the GPS still worked.

A concrete example from the real data

One real well has 5,278 points. The depth is given for the first 1,442 of them (up to PS). You must predict the remaining 3,836 points — and sure enough, the contest asks for exactly 3,836 answers for that well. You predict every foot after the GPS dies.

Step 11 · What's in the files

The two spreadsheets you get for each well

Every well comes as two simple spreadsheets (CSV files). Here's every column in plain English.

Important catch

For the wells you're actually graded on, the horizontal spreadsheet is stripped down to just MD, X, Y, Z, GR, TVT_input. The answer (TVT) and the layer hints are removed. So you really do have to figure the depth out from the glow, the location, and the answer key — exactly like the dead-GPS situation.

Step 12 · A helpful detail

The sideways glow is sharper than the answer key's glow

One practical tip from the experts: the glow measured along the sideways well is more detailed (higher resolution) than the glow in the straight-down answer key. It's like comparing a hi-res photo to a thumbnail — both show the same scene, but one has more detail.

What to do with that

Use the detailed sideways glow from before PS — where you already know the depth — to learn this particular well's glow-to-depth habits, then carry that knowledge forward past PS. It's more reliable than leaning only on the blurry answer key.

Step 13 · Using the neighborhood

The cake is tilted — and neighbors tilt the same way

Picture this The buried cake is slightly tilted. Geologists call that tilt the dip. If you push your straw in the same direction the cake slopes, your depth changes fast. If you push across the slope, the depth barely changes. The compass direction you push is called the azimuth.

And crucially, nearby wells sit in the same tilted cake, so they behave alike. If the neighbor's layer dips down to the east, yours probably does too. So a well next door is a great hint.

Two new words

Dip = how much the layers are tilted. Azimuth = the compass direction the well is drilled. Offset well = a neighboring well, useful as a hint because it sits in the same tilted cake.

Step 14 · The wider view

All the wells together

You're not given one lonely well — you're given a whole field of them. Seeing them together helps you borrow hints from neighbors (Step 13).

Step 15 · How you win

How your guesses get scored

For every predicted point, the contest compares your guessed depth to the real depth and measures the gap:

gap  =  real depth  −  your guess        (at each point)

Then it combines all those gaps into a single score using a formula called RMSE. You don't need the math — just the behavior:

New word

RMSE (Root Mean Squared Error) = roughly, “your typical gap, but big mistakes count extra.” Lower is better. Because mistakes are squared before averaging, one huge miss hurts more than several tiny ones.

So the winning strategy is…

Be roughly right everywhere rather than perfect in spots and wildly wrong elsewhere. Nail the big-picture trend — is the straw going up, down, or staying flat — and avoid the occasional huge blunder. Smooth, sensible guesses beat jittery ones.

What you hand in

A simple two-column file: an id for each point you were asked about, and your guessed depth tvt. One row per point after PS.
